1 Introduction
EYE MOVEMENTanalysis, and more specifically, separation
of fast and slow phases, called saccadic and smooth movements, respectively, are of prime importance both in clinical studies and in research. These two types of ocular
movements are generated by two oculomotor subsystems,
the outputs of which are combined to drive the extraocular
muscles. Over the past ten years numerous complex computer algorithms have been developed to separate automatically the two components (ALLUMet al., !975; BALOH
et al., 1980; BARNES, 1982; MATSUOKAand HARATO,1983;
BALAND et al., 1987). The majority of the proposed algorithms basically use the saccade dynamics (amplitude,
velocity, acceleration and duration) as discriminative criteria. These time-consuming processes, often requiring
offline analysis, are noise-sensitive and necessitate heavy
filtering. They are generally oriented towards slow phase
recognition with fast phase rejection and are dedicated
almost exclusively to the analysis of nystagmus. Ocular
nystagmus consists of alternately slow and fast movements
in opposite directions, induced by appropriate visual or
vestibular stimulation. In the case of sinusoidal vestibular
stimulation, the problem consists of reconstructing the
sinusoidal component of the eye movement. Thus the
method imposes its own limit to nystagmus analysis.

However, eye tracking movements are also of interest
for testing the oculomotor system (STARK et al., 1962;
BAHILL et al., 1980) and oculo-manual co-ordination
(GAuTHIER and HOFFERER,1976; GAUTHIER et al., 1988).
These movements obtained in response to the displacement of a visual target are composed of long smooth
pursuit segments interrupted by correcting saccades. The
analysis of eye tracking movements requires a rather delicate separation of the two components as their velocity
ranges overlap considerably, particularly when tracking
unpredictable target waveforms. The separation method
proposed by MATSUOKAand HARATO (1983) seems to be
very efficient but cannot be applied to a wideband signal.
Thus, the motivation for developing a new procedure was
first, the possibility to analyse a wideband signal without
altering the high-frequency components, and secondly, to
have at hand a fast, reliable and relatively simple algorithm which would even work with a 'noisy' signal.
Thirdly, the need for a fully automatic signal processing
method was also a motivation.
The main objective of this paper is to propose a new
online computer algorithm which is able to detect saccadic
eye movements and their precise beginnings and ends
when sampling the eye position signal at high frequency
(1000 Hz). Furthermore, the method allows the probability
of saccade detection to be quantified. The method was
applied successfully to the analysis of eye movements in an
ocular and manual tracking task. However, a complete
description of the experimental results is beyond the scope
of this present work.

4.4 Algorithm
The principle of the method described above is summarised as follows. An AR model of the smooth pursuit
velocity is identified and used to construct a Kalman filter.
Then the actual eye velocity is compared with the prediction generated by the filter,
The ensuing sequence of innovations allows the detection of fast phase events. The occurrence of a saccade is
confirmed by a chi 2 test. Then, the extrema of the identified saccade (0, 0') are estimated using a Hinkley algorithm. Note that the detection can be achieved using the
Hinkley test; but it is preferable to use the chi 2 test which
gives a significance level. The Hinkley algorithm is only
switched on by the chi 2 test when a saccade has been
detected. Finally, smooth pursuit and saccades are separated,

6 Results

An illustration of the experimental results obtained is
presented in Fig. 6. The typical recording of eye tracking
movements of a random moving target (top trace) shows
the smooth and saccadic movement pattern. After identification of smooth pursuit and saccades, the smooth component was constructed by concatenation of the successive
segments, and respective positional offset corresponding to
the saccade amplitude was added. Smoothing of the signal
was obtained by digital filtering (2Hz) and cubic interpolation. The saccadic component was constructed by
chronological juxtaposition of the saccades. The integrated
sequence of innovations shows saccade detection. Separation of the two components allows quantification of their
respective contribution to eye movements. Gain and phase
of the smooth pursuit as a function of frequency were
analysed further to evaluate the alteration of oculo-manual
co-ordination during hand vibration.
The error of the method verified experimentally was
related to the measurement noise. Indeed, distinction
between small saccades and signal noise was limited by the
resolution of the eye monitoring device, which was 15' of
arc. To obtain the lowest detection threshold false detections, caused by artefacts or abrupt noise variation, were
eliminated automatically according to amplitude and
duration criteria. Blinks were discarded using the same
criteria. Thus, on several recordings containing around 500
saccades, the failure rate for false identification was less
than 1 per cent.

7 Conclusion

Separation of smooth pursuit and saccadic movements
has been achieved using a two-stage process. First, an AR
model of the first derivative of the smooth component was
identified and used to determine a Kalman filter; secondly,
the innovations generated by this filter allowed saccade
detection. The localisation of the saccades was made using
a Hinkley algorithm. The probability of saccade detection
was evaluated by a statistical analysis. The experimental
results confirm the efficiency of this proposed method.
Previous algorithms for saccadic eye movement detection have generally been based on velocity threshold settings (BALOH et el., 1980; BARNES, 1982) or on successive
derivatives of the signal (MATsUOKA and HARATO, 1983).
Although the velocity was used, our algorithm takes into
account both temporal characteristics (AR model) and statistical characteristics (Kalman filter) of the signal. The
main advantages of the present method are as follows.
First, within a relatively large margin of reliability, detection of saccades does not require an a priori knowledge of
their dynamics or of the eye position signal waveform.
Secondly, the method exhibits high immunity to noise and
can be applied to a broadband signal (0-300Hz), which
cannot be the case when successive derivatives are used.
Thirdly, the method is easy to use for online analysis on a
microcomputer as the Kalman filter is synthesised by a
simple recursive algorithm and only one variable
68

(innovation) is tested for saccade detection.
However, once the model is estimated, three parameters
have to be chosen:
(i) the size of the sliding window which is related to the
degree of freedom of the chi square test (N samples)
(ii) the detection threshold to be set according to the
'noise level' of the innovations (this threshold, associated to a probability level, quantifies the significance
of the detection).
(iii) the variances of the state and measurement noises (r,
q) known a priori, are determined empirically by successive trials.
It is worth noting that parameter setting is rapidly
achieved with some experience. The only limitation of the
present algorithm is the use of a model which depends on
the characteristics of the tracking signal and the noise generated by the eye monitoring device. One may, however,
imagine an adaptive procedure for the estimation of these
parameters when they are time-varying.
Automatic eye movement analysis is the aim of numerous investigators interested in the control of eye movements. Despite the large number of algorithms proposed in
the literature, very few have been designed for general
purpose investigation. We think that with minor adaptations the principle of our method can be applied to other
detection problems and more particularly to nystagmus
analysis, which is important for clinical applications.
